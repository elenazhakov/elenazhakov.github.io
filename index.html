Research 01

Heavy Metal Tolerance in Basidiobolus Fungi

Lab
Tabima Lab, Department of Biology, Clark University
Role
Research Assistant
Timeline
January 2026 – present
Advisor
Javier Tabima Restrepo, Ph.D.
Funding
Clark Summer Undergraduate Research Experience (SURE), Summer Fellowship 2026

Fungi are among nature's most effective heavy metal accumulators, wild mushroom species such as porcinis hyperaccumulate lead, cadmium, zinc, and copper from contaminated forest soils. Using those macrofungi for cleanup at scale is impractical: they grow slowly, depend on obligate plant hosts, and need highly specific soil conditions to form the mycelial networks that absorb metal.

The Tabima Lab identified a more tractable candidate in Basidiobolus, a cosmopolitan soil-dwelling microfungus found in forest soils, decomposing leaf litter, aquatic sediments, and the digestive tracts of amphibians. Genomic analysis in the lab revealed specialized gene clusters dedicated to capturing iron; machinery virtually unheard of in related microfungi.

My project tests whether that machinery extends to other metals, the key question for viability as a soil and water bioremediant. I assess growth tolerance in B. ranarum, B. meristosporus and B. haptosporus across concentration gradients of zinc, copper, and cadmium sulfate, and designed the experimental protocols like media preparation, metal stock solutions, fungal culturing, and quantitative analysis of mycelial growth.

Preliminary findings

  • Colony size at the center agar plug held consistent between control and treated plates at 2.5 mM Zn and 100 µM Cu.
  • B. meristosporus exposed to 2.5 mM zinc grew visibly larger than its untreated control, suggesting stimulation rather than stress.
  • Assays continue across higher concentrations and additional metals, including cadmium and lead.

Research 02

Macrocyclic Peptides for Bioorthogonal IEDDA Chemistry

Lab
Nag Lab, Carlson School of Chemistry & Biochemistry, Clark University
Role
Research Assistant
Timeline
September 2024 – January 2026
Collaborators
Rasheda Aktar Samiha (Ph.D. researcher); Arundhati Nag, Ph.D.
Funding
Donald J. Nelson Research Fellowship, Summer Fellowship 2025

The inverse electron demand Diels-Alder (IEDDA) reaction is a bioorthogonal reaction used for bioconjugation, imaging, and screening. Its kinetics depend on how the tetrazine and diene are substituted, and incorporating S,S-disubstituted tetrazines into biomolecules such as peptides is far less synthetically demanding than incorporating aromatic N,N-tetrazines, which makes S,S-tetrazine-linked molecules an attractive route into biocompatible systems.

I synthesized five linear peptides on an Alstra automated synthesizer, cyclized them with tetrazine, and purified and characterized each by HPLC and LC-MS. All five yielded the expected mass and purity profiles. Peptide 1 gave the highest cyclization yield, so the IEDDA optimization work proceeded on that construct; during the reaction the IEDDA adduct hydrolyzed, forming a disulfide bridge between two cyclic species. Additional cyclic analogs are in preparation to widen the study.

The broader project sits in oncology drug discovery. Working alongside Ph.D. researchers, I reviewed and analyzed experimental data across several concurrent lab projects, tracked peptide synthesis progress, and presented the findings at ClarkFEST to a multidisciplinary audience of more than 200 students and faculty.

Methods

  • Automated solid-phase synthesis of linear peptides; on-resin cyclization with tetrazine under argon.
  • Purification and identity confirmation by HPLC and LC-MS for all linear and cyclic species.
  • IEDDA kinetics with moderately reactive dienophiles — norbornene- and cyclopropene-containing molecules — across organic and aqueous conditions.

CAPSTONE PROJECT

The KRAS G12C Story: Undruggable to Approved

Format
Literature review for KRAS G12C 
Focus
Covalent inhibitor development, MAPK signaling, acquired resistance

KRAS G12C, a driver mutation in non-small cell lung cancer, was considered undruggable for decades. This review traces how it became a clinically approved target in roughly ten years, and what happened next.

  1. 2013

    Ostrem et al., Nature — the Switch-II pocket

    A tethering screen with ESI-MS across 480 disulfide fragments found compounds that covalently modify the mutant Cys12 in KRAS(G12C) while sparing wild-type protein. Cell viability assays confirmed mutant-selective activity, though cellular potency was weak.

  2. 2019

    Canon et al. — AMG 510 (sotorasib) reaches patients

    Iterative optimization from Compound 12 through ARS-853 and ARS-1620 produced a roughly 1,000-fold potency gain. AMG 510 showed a ~22-fold higher kobs than ARS-1620, near-complete covalent occupancy with proportional p-ERK suppression in vivo, and objective partial responses in the first dosing cohorts of its first-in-human trial.

  3. 2025

    Kazi et al., J. Thoracic Oncology — answering resistance

    Resistance to sotorasib and adagrasib arrives through secondary KRAS mutations, RTK amplification, and ERK reactivation. Combining FGTI-2734 with sotorasib drove apoptosis in a highly resistant line and produced 62% tumor regression in a patient-derived xenograft.
